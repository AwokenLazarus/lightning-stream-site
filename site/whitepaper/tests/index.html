1Evidence anyone can check

The outside review pointed out that the measurements could not be checked by anyone outside the project. Three things have changed. Public CI builds the tree with its locked dependencies and runs its unit and integration tests on every push and pull request, on GitHub; those tests need no Lightning node, and the lab scripts are not part of it. It passed at the commit Draft 9 described, aaad6da. The reports the lab scripts wrote on their latest runs, on 14 October 2026, are in the tree's reports/ folder, as they were written: every check with what it saw. One of them has a failed check, published with it: in the wallet's end-to-end script the part that kills each process in turn timed out before it reached the seller's node. They are our own runs on one machine, and the figures on this page were mostly written from earlier runs of the same scripts, so a time or a lottery's wins differ between the two. And the tree has a guide for an outside tester, docs/TESTING.md, written from a first run from a fresh clone on a clean Ubuntu 24.04 machine, made as a stranger to the project would make it: the workspace's 707 tests passed, and so did every check of the three lab scripts the guide asks for (the wallet paying a gateway with tickets, 35 of 35; the lottery, 30 of 30; the film demo, 22 of 22). The Lightning lab scripts need Docker and python3 and fetch the rest themselves, so anyone can run them; the scripts for pay with work still need parts that are not published.

2Tickets in the lab

The first table was measured on a three-node regtest lab, one hop: a buyer, one routing node and a seller, all running Lightning Fork v0.21.3-beta-blake2b.17 [61] with default settings, in the regtest lab its author publishes (lightning-fork-lab at commit a5cadc5, on a Bitcoin Knots 29.4.1 BLAKE2b regtest [60]), on one machine, with two channels of 5,000,000 sat. The nodes were driven by scripts, not by the wallet, and all traffic was on loopback, so no figure includes real network latency. A script in the public tree, scripts/ln_ticket_regtest.sh, now reruns these cases on four nodes. Its figures differ a little from the ones below and are in the tree's docs/TICKETS.md.

The four-node script has since run a force close with 100 tickets, which is the figure in the table below. The demo has since paid tickets through two routing nodes.

3The lottery as built

These come from a run on 13 October 2026 of scripts/lottery_regtest.sh, which is in the public tree and makes 30 checks. It ran on a regtest lab, our own nodes: the wallet's signer, its tool server and the gateway as the tree builds them, an ordinary file server, and three Lightning Fork nodes of the version above (a buyer, one routing node, a seller) on one machine, on loopback. The buyer's channel and the seller's inbound channel were opened with 30 slots. There was no Core Lightning node. The settings were the ones the wallet and the gateway ship with, with pieces of 64 KiB. These are one run's figures, and a roll is random, so the wins differ from run to run.

Not measured for the built lottery: sellers on separate machines and nodes (the 100 sellers were 100 gateways beside one seller node); more than 100 sellers; more than one hop, and a direct channel with no routing node; real network latency and mainnet fees; a Core Lightning node; and anything on a small computer. When the buyer's node was killed in the middle of a download, one to three of its payments stayed marked as in flight on that node for good: the wallet keeps those wins booked as spent and does not pay them again, so the seller is not paid for them. The tree's docs/LIMITS.md states this and the other limits under "The lottery".

Building it changed the design, and running it found faults that the tests had not. The plainest: the wallet's limit of 8 payments at a time first let a win go as soon as it was sent, as is right for a ticket. With 100 sellers, 24 wins were then pending at once on the 30-slot channel, and 47 of 313 payments failed. A win now stays inside the limit until it has been paid. The tree's docs/TICKETS.md, section 9, lists what building it changed and what the lab found.

A file cut short with 100 sellers. A later fault showed only when the machine was busy. The gateway bears two unpaid wins from a buyer. At a third, the answer it is sending stands still for the payment, for 10 s at the most, and is then cut short. A file of 64 pieces at one win expected draws three or more wins 8 times in 100, and its answer then stands still at the third until the first is paid. The wallet paid wins strictly in the order it found them, all sellers together, so the first of those three waited its turn behind the other sellers' wins: about 7 s when the machine did nothing else (100 wins at 14 a second), which is inside the 10 s, and more than 10 s when it was busy. The seller then cut the file, and every one of its wins was paid a moment later. With 16 busy loops beside the lab, on its 16 cores, a file was cut in 12 of 15 runs of the 100-seller check; on an idle machine, 13 runs cut none. The fault was not new: it was also there in the tree the lottery was first merged in. The wallet now pays first the wins a seller's answer stands still for. It knows which they are by its own count against the seller's terms, and takes no seller's word for it. Under the same load, every file was whole in 5 runs of 5, and the whole lottery script then passed ten runs out of ten. It is a change of order only: which payments are made, the limit of 8 at a time and every check of an invoice are as before. These are the numbers of one lab on one day (regtest, one machine, our own nodes, one routing node), and the load measures nothing but itself. What the two waits bound beyond that is reasoned from the lab's rate and was not run past 100 sellers: a win no answer waits for would reach the seller's 30 s of grace with about 420 wins waiting, and some 140 wins of the urgent kind at once would use up the 10 s. A slower machine or a slower route lowers the rate, and those numbers shrink with it.

In the design's lab a roll of the first form cost about two millionths of a second for both sides together. As built, a win was paid 221 ms after its payment began (median of 35 payments through one routing node), and each win paid that node 1 sat, which is 1.6% of a 64 sat win. A page bought by a roll took the wallet 3.0 ms a call, against 1.5 ms for a free page: a roll is one more request (on a regtest lab, our own nodes). A roll of the second form cost 16 millionths of a second for each side and 200 to 600 bytes; in 20 runs out of 20 the seller collected its win alone, and the buyer's node showed the payment complete 13 ms after the win. Numbers has the rest.

5A demo: a film paid for as it plays

This paper opens with one buyer and one seller and a streamed film. That case now runs, as a demo, on a regtest. A node runner's media server, Jellyfin as it comes, stands behind the seller's gateway, and a film is bought from it piece by piece through the wallet while a player reads it. Nothing is installed into Jellyfin and none of its settings change. The same file is then bought in pieces from 20 gateways at once, paid by the lottery. A script in the public tree, scripts/demo_film.sh, runs all of it, and the tree's docs/DEMO.md is the guide for a node runner, with the seller's whole setup.

The conditions. Every figure in this section is from one run on 13 October 2026: on a regtest, on one machine, on loopback, between our own Lightning Fork nodes, with channels that carry 30 pending payments. Nothing ran on mainnet or between two people. On one machine everything is a loopback away, so the times say what the software adds and nothing about a real network. Jellyfin was version 10.11.0. The film is Big Buck Bunny, (c) 2008 Blender Foundation, www.bigbuckbunny.org, licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 3.0: 276 MB, 10:34 long, 1080p, 3.5 Mbit/s. It was priced at 100 sat a MiB and paid in tickets of 1,000 sat. That price is a number chosen so that a ticket lasts about half a minute of this film. It is not a market price. The rules of tickets and of the lottery were the shipped ones, but the demo's driver changes some other settings: the owner names every seller, so nothing in the demo is paid as a stranger; caps on rates and sessions are raised for a lab where every buyer comes from one address; and the rule that keeps small calls off the lottery is turned off for the 20 sellers, so that their rolls can be shown. docs/DEMO.md lists each change, and says where the script gives no reason for one.

On the buyer's side stood the wallet and a small paying proxy, a demonstration script that a player reads from in place of the seller. Each request the player makes becomes one call of the wallet's pay tool, so the wallet pays as the film plays and stops when the player stops asking. The proxy holds no key and no ticket secret. The film was played as HLS, in segments of about six seconds, and priced by the byte.

The same file from 20 sellers. The film's file, 263.3 MiB, was cut into 1,054 pieces of 256 KiB at 1 sat a piece and bought from 20 gateways at once. A piece costs far less than a ticket, and 20 sellers are more than tickets can be held with from one 30-slot channel, so the wallet paid by rolls: one roll in 64 wins, and a win is an ordinary payment of 64 sat. There were 1,054 rolls and 14 wins, where 16.5 were expected. The buyer paid 896 sat for pieces priced at 1,053 sat; the run before it paid 576 sat. Nine of the 20 sellers were paid nothing: each had sold about 52 sat of pieces, which is less than one win. Routing fees were 14 sat. Never more than 4 payments were on the buyer's channel at once, nothing was held between wins, and no payment failed. Every piece was checked against its hash, and the file was whole in 3 s. That is the lottery as it is: exact on average and uneven in one run, for the buyer and for each seller. The 20 gateways stood beside one seller node (on a regtest, one machine, our own nodes, one routing node).

What the demo does not show

A person watching
Nobody watched this film on a screen. The players of the run were ffmpeg, for the first frame, and a script that asks for segments as an HLS player does: it keeps 12 s ahead, pauses and seeks. mpv, VLC and a browser were not tried.
A stock Jellyfin app paying
It could not, in this demo: the player was handed the film's address. Since version 0.2.0 Jellyfin's own web app, with its logins, browsing and range requests, runs whole through Stream; see 4K and HDR and the 0.2.0 round.
An AI model as the agent
The agent of the demo is a script that speaks MCP with the wallet's tools.
Real coins, or two people
It is a regtest, on one machine, between our own nodes. Letting people stream from a seller needs a shared test network or mainnet, a gateway reachable from outside behind TLS, and somebody's decision to expose a home server.
Seeking inside what was paid for
A viewer who seeks back pays for those segments again.
What the limits already say
A seller that takes a ticket and serves nothing keeps at most one ticket, what is left of the last ticket stays with the seller, and a buyer that never pays a win was served about one win's worth.

For a seller: what a buyer can reach is decided by the gateway's routes, one film each, so the service's own port must never be published, only the gateway's. In the demo a second film on the same server, and the server's own pages, answered 404 through the gateway.

Bodies and feesEvery body matched its file. No routing fees: the channel is direct. Over both runs the buyer's side of the channel went from 99,056 to 94,672 sat, and the seller's from 0 to 4,384 sat: what the two runs paid. No payment failed.

The four faults and their fixes. The cap counted coins on chain, which a payment cannot spend, so any cap below the node's whole balance refused every payment; it now counts the channels and what is in flight. The gateway did not say in its 402 that it holds tickets only after a prepaid one, so the wallet tried held tickets first; the terms now say so, and the wallet buys the prepaid ticket first. The gateway looked at a win's invoice only when the buyer next asked for something; it now looks on every round of its janitor. And what is left of the last ticket stays with the seller when a session ends: that is by design and less than one ticket, and the wallet now reports it. The fixes are in the public tree, with tests.

What came first. The box was never the limit. The first limits were the shop's default caps in that build, 30 buyers at once and 64 open connections (48 from one address), which turned buyers away before they paid; the release raises them to 100 sessions and 512 connections, and a shop behind a tunnel is now limited as a whole. After the caps comes the bandwidth to the buyers. The run also found that a full service could turn away buyers it was already serving, and that a buyer refused at the last moment could have paid a ticket first; in 0.2.0 a busy service refuses new buyers before they pay, and those it is already serving stay served. Over https and a direct link Stream adds little: under about 1.5 s at the start and 0.5 s at a seek, with stalls and dropped frames as without it, up to 35 Mbit/s and through 5 to 10 ticket refills; no stall lined up with a refill. The picture is decided by Jellyfin and the browser. Chrome without an HEVC decoder gets every HEVC clip, HDR or not, converted to 4K H.264, which took Jellyfin 8 to 11 processor cores; a 2 to 4 core seller's box cannot do it as the film plays without hardware transcoding, and without tone mapping HDR looks washed out. Real HDR and Dolby Vision need a player that decodes them, such as Jellyfin's TV and phone apps, mpv or VLC.

A relay over a slow path is the weak case. From lab machines behind a VPN, 327 ms from the relay, the web client first signed in and then fell back to its login page, and nothing played. Four faults were found and fixed for 0.2.0: every request asked its price before paying, two round trips before the first byte; the wallet held a finished answer open for its last receipt; a refusal the shop did not sign stopped the session; and a signed refusal of one page was taken for "sign in first". After the fixes, on the same path, a 4K film (7.5 Mbit/s) played in 7 runs of 7 that reached playback: it started after 14.6 to 24.6 s and stalled 5 to 7 times, against 1.0 to 1.5 s and one short stall straight from Jellyfin on the same machine. The web client took about 35 s to load the first time over that path. Money agreed in every run. With 800 ms added to every packet the link did not come up at all. Starting slowly over a slow relay remains the main open item for web apps.

11Hashrate under load

In the lab, 30 October 2026. One hashmaker and 25 hashtakers renting at once, 3 simulated miners each for 30 s, with a stand-in for payment (no Lightning nodes), in a release build. Every rental got 532 to 537 of the 540 shares its miners could deliver, 98 to 99%; the longest gap in a miner's shares was 666 ms against a 2,000 ms job; no share was rejected and no miner was rented twice. An earlier run found miners behind one address bouncing between rentals; they now move once each way. A buyer is charged only for shares its own pool accepted.
